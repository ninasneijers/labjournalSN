Conclusion - Exploratory

by: Nina Sneijers

1 Conclusion

This study has investigated to what extent students’ social networks are segregated on parental education level and what the effect of having parents with the same educational level is on a student’s social satisfaction. The data used to investigate this question was from one of the Michigan Learning Communities, consisting of 121 students. For analysis, Stochastic Action Oriented Models were used. This study has posed four research questions:

  1. “To what extent is the MLC network segregated based on parental education level?”
  2. “To what extent do reciprocated friendships among students with parents with the same educational level occur?”
  3. “To what extent do reciprocated friendships affect students’ social satisfaction throughout the year?”
  4. “To what extent do reciprocated friendships among students with parents with the same education level affect their social satisfaction throughout the year?”

The first question took a macro-perspective on the network. I used Moran’s I and Coleman’s homophily index to answer this question and concluded that the first hypothesis “The MLC network is segregated on parental education level” should be rejected as the statistics did not indicate that this was the case. This is interesting, because it contrasts what other studies have found previously (see: (Zwier and Geven 2023)). However, this study took place in an American context and additionally, other studies have also taken into account the parental income-level. This finding indicates that income-level might play a bigger role in SES-segregation.

The second question posed a micro-perspective and specifically looked into homophily among reciprocated ties. The results seemed to indicate that students were rather less likely than more likely to have reciprocated ties with other students whose parents had the same educational level, though this was not significant. Therefore, the second hypothesis “Reciprocated ties are more likely to exist among students with parents with the same educational level” was also rejected. For the control variables gender and ethnicity, this was also not the case. As reciprocity itself was positive and significant, this shows that if a tie is send, it is likely that it well be reciprocated, no matter the characteristics of the other person.

The third question assessed the effect of the network on behaviour. The behaviour, in this case, was social satisfaction. Results showed that the effect of reciprocated ties on social satisfaction was close to zero, indicating that reciprocation in friendship has no influence on social satisfaction. This leads to the third hypothesis, “Reciprocated ties positively affect students’ social satisfaction” also being rejected.

The last question assessed an interaction effect of reciprocated ties between students whose parents have the same educational level. This effect was positive, but not significant and therefore also rejected. Additionally, the Goodness-of-Fit of this model was worse than the Goodness-of-Fit of the model of question three. This indicates that there are other network or interaction effects that affect change in behaviour, that are more influential than these characteristics. In both models, the influence of the quadratic shape effect remained the biggest, indicating that students whose social satisfaction is below the mean are likely to move towards the mean.

This study also has several limitations. Firstly, this study has not distinctly looked at students whose parents are higher educated versus students whose parents are not higher educated, meaning that the study has looked into, overall, does homophily in reciprocated ties affect social satisfaction. It could be the case that this homophily does not have an influence on students whose parents are higher educated and that their social satisfaction depends on other things, but that it does have an influence on students whose parents are not higher educated. The study by (Melian et al. 2026) suggests that this is indeed the case. The Michigan Learning Communities prioritises first generation students and shape the program towards their needs (Rambaran, Ashwin 2025), which could have affected their social satisfaction. However, because I was limited to only one learning community, the amount of students whose parents had not enjoyed higher education was relatively small, which would have made it harder to assess the effect of homophily of reciprocated ties on their friendships. This is why I have chosen to assess the overall effect of same parental education level homophily on social satisfaction. Therefore, a future study could look into the effects specifically for students whose parents have not enjoyed higher education. Additionally, the study could then assess multiple learning communities, so network differences can be assessed.

Secondly, the Goodness-of-Fit models for the Outdegree Distribution and for the TriadCensus distribution did not reach values that are desirable. This indicates that perhaps more or other effects should have been included in the models to create a better fit. Additionally, the Goodness-of-Fit of the Behavioural distribution worsened and overestimated the amount of people with very high social satisfaction, indicating that here as well, other effects might were more relevant for explaining changes in behaviour than the effects that were used in this study.

Another limitation of the study is that students that the indegree ties of students that were missing in one of the two waves, were coded as a non-nomination in these waves. This could have led to biases in certain statistics, such as transitivity, and reciprocity, which was my effect of interest. When looking at the network graph provided in the descriptives, it can be seen that some of the students who were big tie-senders and receivers in wave 1, were missing in wave 2. Would their incoming ties have been included, it perhaps would have lead to different results.

While this study has not yielded significant results and knows its limitations, this does not mean that the social network perspective is not relevant. Firstly, a social network perspective allowed me to assess whether segregation takes place in a network and account for this. Would this study be conducted over multiple communities and for a longer period of time, for example segregation helps us to assess differences in a network and control for this. Perhaps a more segregated network also has a different culture and different effects on students who do or do not befriend other students whose parents have enjoyed a similar education level.

Additionally, a social network perspective gives the opportunity to assess different types of networks, for example, the amount of ties ones has, how many triads it consists of and how many ties are reciprocated. (Wittner et al. 2023) has shown that for first generation students, it matters if their network is for example close-knit or “have-it-all”, as this influences how they manage at universities. A social network perspective would enable one to take differences in these networks into account in their analysis.

Lastly, this study has taken some control-variables into account, such as the average social satisfaction of ones friends, as this can also influence ones social satisfaction. A linear regression analysis, for example, would have not been able to do this, while friends can be of important influential factors on how someone is feeling.
