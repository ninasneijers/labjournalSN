Final Project - Introduction

by: Nina Sneijers

1 Introduction

Starting one’s first year at university brings quite a lot of challenges. Students often move-out of their family homes, have to get acquainted with a new environment, navigate new social contexts and learn how to study more independently than in high school. Challenges are especially present for students whose parents or guardians have not enjoyed higher education themselves, as these students often lack the practical support of someone who can assist them with navigating through this new type of environment. These students not only face structural inequalities that make them less prepared for the academic world (Melian et al. 2026), but once entering college, they are also confronted with a different type of world than what their family has prepared them for, with different cultural expectations that give them a harder time accessing the right skills, knowledge and resources needed to navigate this world Stephens et al. (2014).

These students often experience a lack of social support within universities and feel less like they belong there than students with parents who have enjoyed university Wittner et al. (2023). This, while a positive correlation exists between belonging, motivation and enjoyment and a combination of these three affects the academic achievement of students (Pedler et al. 2022). Thus, while these students are already facing more challenges than others at universities, their chances of better academic achievement are also affected by how much they feel like they belong.

This does, however, not mean that all first generation students experience the same level of difficulties at university. This also depends on, for example, what their network at university looks like in terms of socio-emotional support and instrumental support (Wittner et al. 2023). What especially seems to be of importance is what the friendship network of these students looks like. Melian et al. (2025) show that for first-generation students, their mental and social well-being was consistently linked to their friendship networks, while this was not the case for other student groups.

However, there are differences in the type of friendship networks that are positive for social well-being. (Wittner et al. 2023) shows that the type of network in terms of closeness affects how FGS manage at universities and what influence it has on social well-being. Additionally, there can be questioned how friendship-network similarity influences the well-being of students. For example, in terms of racial diversity, it is shown that for white students, there is a benefit in developing more diverse friendships, while this is not necessarily the case for students with a migration background [Janke et al. (2024)](Salusky et al. 2024). In the more general population, it is shown that there are benefits to having a more heterogeneous rather than a homogeneous network, as it leads to higher levels of social well-being (Ramos et al. 2024). Though, while in the more general population it seems to lead to more positive effects, for first generation students, this does not necessarily seem to be the case. For this group, a more socioeconomically diverse network is linked to lower levels of social well-being. They are likely facing more and different challenges in these heterogeneous contexts, as they are navigating between multiple cultural contexts and managing feelings of mismatch Stephens et al. (2014).

Previous studies have thus already looked into the relationship between friendship diversity and social well-being for students with parents that do not have a university background. However, these students lack the social network perspective that this study will take into account. For example, Ramos et al. (2024) mention that their study is limited by the fact that friendship is a subjective experience. A social network perspective makes it possible to look into whether friendships are reciprocated, meaning that if you see someone as your friend, there can be also considered whether you see this person as a friend. Taking this into account potentially yield a more complete picture between diversity in friendship networks and social well-being, as it is likely that answered friendships make someone feel more socially satisfied than one-directed friendships.

Additionally, while Melian et al. (2025) have already looked into the influence of a socioeconomically diverse network on social satisfaction of first generation students, they did not take into account the interdependencies that a social network analysis can take into account. This perspective allows me to look into influence effects of social networks, because the people with whom we form social relations influence our attitudes, behaviours and also future relations (Tolsma and Hofstra 2022). For this study, that means that a social network perspective allows me to control for assimilation effects of social satisfaction, meaning that the social satisfaction ratings of students befriended with each other may become more alike over time.

Thus, a social network perspective allows me to look into this topic in new and different ways than previous studies have done and different questions emerge from this. The study aims to answer four research questions: 1. “To what extent is the MLC network segregated based on parental education level?” 2. “To what extent do reciprocated friendships among students with parents with the same educational level occur?” 3. “To what extent do reciprocated friendships affect students’ social satisfaction throughout the year?” 4. “To what extent do reciprocated friendships among students with parents with the same education level affect their social satisfaction throughout the year?”
